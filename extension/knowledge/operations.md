# Operations management knowledge (built-in guide)

General operations leadership knowledge for analysing sources and writing about them.
Company-specific definitions and targets come from the uploaded standard work and take priority over this guide.

## Controllable vs uncontrollable
- **Controllable failure**: a miss the team could have prevented with its own people, planning, process or equipment.
  Examples: short staffing from poor scheduling or unmanaged call-outs, process not followed, late build-up,
  missorts, equipment not ready, missed cut-off, poor shift handover, missing communication.
- **Uncontrollable**: caused outside the team's control. Examples: weather, late inbound aircraft, ATC delays,
  customer or carrier system outages, upstream misroutes, regulatory holds.
- Many events are mixed. Say which part was controllable. Do not label something controllable without evidence in the sources.

## Root cause
- Separate the **event** (what happened), the **direct cause** (what made it happen) and the **root cause** (why the
  direct cause was possible). Fix the root cause, not only the symptom.
- **5 Whys**: ask "why" until you reach a process or system cause the team can change. Stop at a person's name; go on to why the process let it happen.
- **Fishbone** categories: People, Process (Methods), Equipment, Materials, Environment, Measurement.
- **Corrective action** fixes this instance. **Preventive action** stops it recurring. Each needs an owner and a due date.
- Look for **repeat** issues across days, shifts and gateways. A repeat means the previous fix did not hold.

## Performance language
- KPI: measured against a target over a period, with a trend. Always state target, actual, period, direction.
- Leading indicators (staffing fill, training completion, near-miss reports) predict lagging ones (OTP, injuries, damage).
- Report variance: actual vs target, vs last week, vs same period last year where available.
- Watch for denominators: a rate (per 1,000 packages, per 200,000 hours) is fairer than a raw count across gateways of different sizes.

## Safety leadership
- Safety comes first in every report and agenda.
- Near misses are valuable: they show risk before injury. Praise reporting; never punish honest reporting.
- **Just Culture**: separate honest error (console and fix the system), at-risk behaviour (coach), and reckless behaviour (discipline).
- **Hierarchy of controls**, most to least effective: eliminate, substitute, engineering controls, administrative controls (procedures, training, signs), PPE.
- OSHA recordables: injuries needing treatment beyond first aid, days away, restricted work, and others defined by OSHA. First aid only is not recordable. Use the company's own classification when it is in the library.

## Staffing and attendance
- Headcount vs required (plan) vs actual on shift. Fill rate = actual / required.
- Call-outs and no-shows: track by shift and day; patterns (Mondays, nights, after payday) point to causes.
- Overtime covers gaps but costs money and raises fatigue risk; sustained high OT is a staffing signal.
- Cross-training builds flexibility for short-staffed shifts.

## Continuous improvement
- **PDCA**: Plan, Do, Check, Act. **DMAIC**: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control.
- Standard work: the current best known way, written down, trained and audited. Deviations are learning chances.
- Gemba: go and see the process where the work happens.
- A good action is SMART: specific, measurable, assigned, realistic, time-bound.

## Shift handover
- What happened, what is open, what is at risk on the next shift, safety items, equipment status, staffing.
- Written and verbal. The incoming lead confirms they understand the open items.

## Writing about operations
- Use SBAR for escalations: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.
- Lead with impact on safety, the customer, cost and people, in that order unless the reader asks otherwise.
- Every problem statement should end in an action with an owner and a date.
- When the sources disagree, say so and name both sources. Do not pick one silently.
- When "connecting dots" across sources: look for the same issue, gateway, shift, equipment or person-role appearing more than once;
  timing (one event before another); a stated plan vs what happened; gaps between standard work and practice.
